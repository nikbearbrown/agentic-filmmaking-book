## Learning Outcomes

Format: by the end of the chapter, the learner will be able to...

**Introduction: Can You Make Your Film Now?**
- (Understand) explain who does what: the agent generates, logs and prices options; the filmmaker keeps, rejects or changes them.
- (Understand) describe the cost curve (words, draft stills, hero stills, motion, sound, cut) and why you never pay for motion to find out what something looks like.
- (Apply) set up a project folder, a production log and an agent session that record every decision, cost and job ID.
- (Apply) make a three-shot micro-film end to end and log what departed from what they intended. *(the pebble; see Three-Act Learning Arc)*

**Ch 1: How Films Tell Stories**
- (Remember) identify shot sizes, angles, mise-en-scène elements, edit types and diegetic or non-diegetic sound in a clip, in words they can type to an agent.
- (Analyze) explain how two juxtaposed shots create meaning that neither holds alone.
- (Analyze) map each of the five stages of production to the human work and the AI work it involves.
- (Evaluate) argue whether a generated image can do what Bazin claimed for the photographic image, using one example.

**Ch 2: Story: Idea, Conflict and Structure**
- (Apply) write a logline and a 25-word premise with an explicit conflict and need.
- (Create) develop a step outline for a short film in three-act form.
- (Evaluate) audit an AI-proposed outline against a short-film cliché list and rewrite its weakest beats from their own material.

**Ch 3: The Screenplay**
- (Apply) format a short screenplay to industry standard.
- (Analyze) find on-the-nose dialogue and buried exposition, and revise toward subtext.
- (Create) convert the script into a shot table an agent can generate from, using a template with blanks for their own decisions.
- (Evaluate) answer each flag the agent raises (period, scale, rights) with a logged decision.

**Ch 4: The Visual Story**
- (Understand) explain contrast and affinity across Block's visual components.
- (Create) write look rules (light, palette, camera, what is banned in frame) that override the model's default look in every prompt.
- (Analyze) compare the same scene made under opposite look rules, or by two people, and name the decisions that made them differ.
- (Evaluate) judge a set of generated frames against their own look rules.

**Ch 5: The Frame: Composition, Angle and Lens**
- (Apply) specify a frame by shot size, angle, lens and depth of field to override the model's default framing.
- (Analyze) compare how two image models render the same camera instructions.
- (Evaluate) decide whether a still matches the frame they intended, and keep or reject it with a reason.

**Ch 6: Light and Color**
- (Understand) explain hard and soft light, key, fill and back light, and motivated light.
- (Create) design a lighting and color scheme for a scene.
- (Apply) hold that scheme across a sequence of draft stills and repair the stills that drift.

**Ch 7: Designing the World: Sets, Props, Costume and Characters**
- (Create) build a character reference sheet (four or five angles) and a hero-prop reference.
- (Analyze) find where generated designs depart from the filmmaker's intent (identity drift, wrong details, continuity).
- (Evaluate) flag where a design departs from the period, and record each departure as the filmmaker's choice.
- (Evaluate) decide whether a likeness may be used, based on documented consent.

**Ch 8: Storyboards and the Shot List**
- (Apply) storyboard a whole scene in draft stills, numbered, with arrows, labels and a map.
- (Evaluate) review the storyboard frame by frame against their intent, logging each keep, reject or question.
- (Create) choose which frames get hero stills to become first frames, and write the linked shot list.

**Ch 9: Producing: Budget, Schedule and Crew**
- (Apply) break a script down into its production elements.
- (Create) build a shot-by-shot budget that prices draft stills, hero stills and motion separately, with a test budget.
- (Evaluate) decide which shots to generate, shoot live, or cut, and justify each against budget and story.

**Ch 10: Movement: Camera and Action**
- (Understand) distinguish motivated from unmotivated camera movement.
- (Apply) generate clips from approved hero stills, each with one stated camera move that overrides the model's default motion.
- (Evaluate) review clips on four-frame contact sheets and reject motion that departs from their intent.

**Ch 11: Directing Performance and Coverage**
- (Apply) plan coverage for a two-character dialogue scene with a floor plan that keeps the 180-degree rule.
- (Create) generate that coverage with consistent characters and matching eyelines, drafts before hero stills before motion.
- (Evaluate) judge performances across takes and choose one, with reasons.

**Ch 12: Sound: Dialogue, Voice, Effects and Music**
- (Understand) explain diegetic and non-diegetic sound, and why room tone and ADR matter.
- (Apply) produce voice, ambience, effects and music for a scene and mix them to a loudness target.
- (Evaluate) keep a rights and consent record for every voice and music source, and reject any source that fails it.

**Ch 13: Editing: Continuity, Time and Rhythm**
- (Apply) direct the agent to assemble a scene from the shot table.
- (Create) cut the assembly to a fine cut by giving cut notes to the agent directly.
- (Evaluate) justify each cut against Murch's rule of six.

**Ch 14: VFX, Compositing and Finishing**
- (Apply) composite a generated element into a live-action plate, or a live element into a generated shot.
- (Apply) finish a cut: titles and overlays added in post, upscaling last.
- (Evaluate) label, shot by shot, what is real and what is generated.

**Ch 15: Distribution, Festivals and Disclosure**
- (Apply) prepare delivery files, captions, metadata and synthetic-media disclosure for a release.
- (Create) build a festival and fundraising plan, with a pitch reel.
- (Evaluate) check a film's credits and rights record against festival and platform rules.

**Capstone: The Opening Scene**
- (Create) make and release the opening scene of their own film through the whole pipeline.
- (Evaluate) check the film and the production log against the self-review checklist: every decision, cost, rejected take and disclosure.

## Sequencing Model and Justification

**Primary model: simple to complex, ordered by cost.** Each part adds one expensive dimension to the film: Part I words, Part II space and light (stills), Part III time and sound (clips), Part IV the whole (the cut and the release). Within Part II, stills come in two tiers: cheap draft stills to explore, then one paid hero still per approved shot, because the video can only be as clear as the still it starts from. A learner who pays for credits makes the cheapest mistakes first. This is how the course keeps them out of the rabbit hole of bad decisions.

**Secondary model: a spiral of the review pass.** The +1 judgment returns in every part, each time against the filmmaker's intent, with a new layer:

| Return | Chapter | What is judged | New layer this time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ch 2 | an AI outline | meaning: is the story yours, or a cliché? |
| 2 | Ch 4 to 8 | draft stills | space, light and identity in one frame; period departures logged as choices |
| 3 | Ch 10 | clips | time and physics across the frames of a clip |
| 4 | Ch 11 | coverage | identity, eyeline and screen direction across shots |
| 5 | Ch 13 | the cut | rhythm and emotion across the whole scene, through the learner's own cut notes |

**Where the model is most likely to break:** Ch 11. Directing performance needs everything before it, and it is where the models are weakest and where no real run exists yet.

**Transition chapter:** Ch 9 (Producing), the pivot from cheap to expensive work. Before crossing it, the learner has reviewed draft stills, chosen hero stills, look rules, character references and a budget.

## Three-Act Learning Arc

**Length:** self-paced with no fixed length, so the 12-to-14-chapter semester target does not apply. 15 chapters plus an Introduction stand. [Follows from Bear's deployment answer, 2026-11-16]

**The pebble (Merrill's first principle).** Recommended: in the Introduction, before any theory, the learner makes a three-shot micro-film with the agent: a few draft stills, one hero still, one clip, and a cut with a voice line. They see the whole pipeline once, and see where it departs from what they intended, which creates the need for every chapter after. At logged prices that is about 2 credits per draft still plus 26 for the cheapest logged clip, plus the hero still. [NEEDS HUMAN INPUT: hero-still price] [INFERRED — Blueprint recommendation]

**Act One: Establish (Intro to Ch 3).**
- *Inciting question:* what is my film, and can I see it before I spend anything?
- *By the end:* a script and a shot table, with a logged decision for every flag.

**Act Two: Build (Ch 4 to Ch 12).**
- *Tools it provides:* look rules, frame and light vocabulary, character references, the draft storyboard and its review, hero stills, the budget, image-to-video, coverage, the mix.
- *Hardest moment:* Ch 11, consistent performance across generated coverage.
- *By the end:* every shot of the scene exists as an approved clip with its sound, inside the budget.

**Act Three: Apply (Ch 13 to Ch 15, capstone).**
- *Where it comes together:* the cut, the finish, the release.
- *What the learner produces:* a released opening scene, honestly labelled, with its production log; the first win that can help raise money for the rest.

**Transitions:**
- *Act One to Act Two:* the shot table exists and every flag in it has a logged decision.
- *Act Two to Act Three:* approved clips and a soundtrack exist, within budget, with job IDs logged.

**Arc statement:** this course takes the learner from *having a vision for a film* to *releasing a finished, honestly labelled opening scene they directed*, by first planning it in words, then seeing it whole in cheap draft stills, then paying for hero stills and motion only where the drafts have earned it.

## Front-Loading Decisions

- **Introduction as Chapter 0:** agent setup, project folder, production log, accounts and credits, and the pebble.
- **Film vocabulary in Ch 1:** shot sizes, angles, edit types and sound types, so the shot table in Ch 3 can use them.
- **Every term defined at first use,** in plain words, throughout. Audio terms (loudness, ducking) get an embedded explanation in Ch 12, not a chapter.
