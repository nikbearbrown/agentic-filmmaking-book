## Concept

Bear's request, in his words (2026-11-16): *"It's basically going to be a filmmaking course, so like a general filmmaking book. But then for each chapter, we talk about how AI can do that thing, audio, video, storyboarding, et cetera."*

Thesis: the filmmaker conducts; the agent plays. The agent generates, logs and prices options; every choice of what to keep, reject or change belongs to the filmmaker. The proof is that two people can take the same idea, run the same tools and make completely different films, because the difference lives in their decisions, not in the software. The course teaches the tech so it stops being the obstacle, and never makes the creative calls for the reader.

**Two consequences that run through the introduction and every chapter's AI half:**

- **The agent is the hands.** What the course removes is the human translator standing between the filmmaker's vision and the agent, not the delegation. The pitch is "direct it yourself", not "do it yourself".
- **Bear's role ends at teaching.** Bear will not be Galen's hands, or relay "change this, update that" between Galen and the agent. The course exists so the filmmaker talks to the agent directly.

**The question behind it** (from [/Users/bear/Documents/CoWork/bear-textbooks/books/ai-filmmaking/README.md](/Users/bear/Documents/CoWork/bear-textbooks/books/ai-filmmaking/README.md)): can a filmmaker with no Hollywood budget now make the film they have carried for years? Galen has spent most of his time raising money. The realistic first win is a finished opening scene that helps raise money for the rest. That is what the capstone builds.

Target reader: anyone who wants to make AI films. Many already carry a clear vision in their head; what they lack is the technical skill to get an AI to produce it. Comfortable chatting with an AI; no film school, no editing software, no camera required. The craft half of each chapter is what separates this course from tool tutorials. Galen is the running case, not the target reader: an old-school filmmaker with UCLA training who is new to AI, whose judgment models the +1. [CONFIRMED: Bear, 2026-11-16]

**Deployment: a self-paced online course, with no fixed length.** Not a semester course, not a workshop. In Bear's words, it is for people who *"have a dream of making films"* and want to start making their vision *without "going down a rabbit hole of bad decisions"*. [CONFIRMED: Bear, 2026-11-16]

**Writing rule:** plain and practical. Every term is defined in plain words the first time it appears.

## Chapter anatomy (every chapter, same five parts)

1. **The craft, taught as vocabulary.** Shot size, lens, light quality, period detail and the rest of the filmmaker's language, taught as the way to describe the picture in your head precisely enough for a model to produce it. Not film school for its own sake: each term earns its place by changing what the model makes.
2. **The agent does it.** What the model does by default, and how to override it. The agent is the hands; the filmmaker directs it directly, in their own words. Model and tool names appear only in a dated **"Tools as of"** box, so the chapter does not age with them.
3. The +1. What only the filmmaker can judge: where the output departs from what the filmmaker intended. Named failures come from real work (a ghost second sail, a spyglass where the script says monocle). A departure from history, or from someone else's taste, is not a failure: the agent flags it once, the filmmaker decides, and the decision goes in the production log.
4. **On the set: Galen's opening.** What was actually done so far, with the real files, and whose hands did it. Where something has not been run yet, the chapter gives Galen the exact session to run, never a description of what he would decide.
5. **Exercises.** At least three; one at Apply level or higher. Every exercise ends in a file the student can open. Templates have blanks for the reader's own decisions; there are no finished prompts to copy. Galen's prompts are shown as examples of his decisions, never as defaults for the reader.

## Sequencing model and three-act arc

**Sequencing model: the cost curve.** The course follows the production order (development, pre-production, production, post, release), which every pantry book also uses. The agentic twist is that the order is also the cost order: a word costs almost nothing, a logged still about 2 credits, a logged 8-second clip 26 to 128 credits depending on the model. Generation costs real money. [NEEDS HUMAN INPUT: dollar price per credit, so the course can state costs in money.] Bear's first rule says it: *work from cheap to expensive; fix problems at the still, because a bad still makes a bad clip.* So the frame, the light and the look (classically production work) move forward into pre-production, where they are decided on stills before any motion is paid for.

Within the stills there are two tiers. Explore with fast, cheap image models (draft stills); pay for a high-quality model only for the hero still that becomes a clip's first frame, because the video can only be as clear as the still it starts from. The rule students carry: never pay for motion to find out what something looks like. Worked example: the Wrong Ship. A trireme that a draft still would have exposed for a few credits reached two finished films before it was caught. [NEEDS HUMAN INPUT: credits spent on Films 1 and 2 before the ship was caught.]

[NEEDS HUMAN INPUT: logged price per draft still and per hero still, so Ch 9 can budget the two tiers separately.]

**Anchor exhibit: the Wrong Ship.** Asked for an ancient Greek ship, the generation went to a textbook trireme with two sails and banks of oars, and that ship appeared in Films 1 and 2. Galen's ship, as the later stills show, has a curl and a beak and a single bank of oars. Only he could catch the difference. The notes on the review slides ("whole ship · curl and beak · adults", "warm wood, oars biting, too many aboard", "same hull · no lightning") are the relay loop this course is designed to end. In the course, notes like these are what the filmmaker types to the agent directly. The exhibit appears in the introduction, in Ch 7, and in the cost chapter (Ch 9).

**Three-act arc:**

- **Act One: Establish (Intro to Ch 3).** What is the film? The student learns the vocabulary of how films make meaning, finds a story, and writes it as a script an agent can generate from.
- **Act Two: Build (Ch 4 to Ch 12).** The hard middle. First the whole film as stills (look, frame, light, world, storyboard, budget), then motion, performance and sound. Ch 9 (Producing) is the gate in the middle: nothing moves until the stills are approved and the money is planned.
- **Act Three: Apply (Ch 13 to Ch 15).** The student cuts, finishes and releases a film, with credits, disclosure and rights in order.
- **Capstone.** The opening scene of the student's own film, made through the whole pipeline, with a production log.

Arc statement: takes the student from *having a vision for a film* to *releasing a finished, honestly labelled opening scene they directed*, by first planning it in words, then seeing the whole film in cheap draft stills, then paying for hero stills and motion only where the drafts have earned it.
